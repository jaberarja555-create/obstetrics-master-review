Cardiac output rises by about 40%The stroke volume rises first; later the heart rate also rises, by 10–15 beats per minute. Cardiac output rises again during labour and is highest immediately after delivery. For this reason a woman with heart disease is most likely to develop heart failure at 24–28 weeks, during labour and just after delivery.
Peripheral resistance fallsProgesterone dilates the blood vessels, so the blood pressure (BP) falls in the second trimester and returns to the booking level by term. A BP higher than the booking value is therefore abnormal.
Venous pressure in the legs risesThe enlarged uterus presses on the inferior vena cava (IVC) and the iliac veins. This causes ankle oedema, varicose veins and haemorrhoids (piles).
Supine hypotensionAfter 20 weeks, lying flat on the back allows the uterus to compress the IVC. Less blood returns to the heart, so the woman may feel dizzy, her BP falls and the fetus may become distressed. This is prevented by a left lateral tilt of 15°.
Heart and ECGThe heart is pushed upwards and to the left, so the apex beat moves to the 4th intercostal space. A soft ejection systolic murmur and a third heart sound are normal. The ECG may show left axis deviation and a small Q wave in lead III. A diastolic murmur is never normal and must be investigated.

4. Blood and clotting

Physiological anaemia. The plasma volume rises by about 45%, but the red cell mass rises by only about 25%. The blood is therefore diluted, and the haemoglobin (Hb) concentration falls to about 11 g/dL, while the mean cell volume (MCV) stays normal. This is called physiological anaemia, or haemodilution. The total iron requirement of pregnancy is about 1000 mg. The white cell count (WBC) rises to as much as 15 × 10⁹/L, and higher in labour; the platelet count falls slightly. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) is normally high in pregnancy, so it is not a useful test.

The hypercoagulable state. The levels of fibrinogen and of clotting factors VII, VIII and X rise, the natural anticoagulant protein S falls, and the breakdown of clots (fibrinolysis) is reduced. These changes protect the mother from bleeding at delivery. Together with slow venous flow in the legs, however, they increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) 4–6 times. The risk is highest in the weeks after delivery.
