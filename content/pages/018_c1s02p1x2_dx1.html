Amenorrhoea is not always pregnancy

Other causes of amenorrhoea include stress, weight loss, heavy exercise, breastfeeding, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), thyroid disease, high prolactin, injectable contraception (DMPA) and the menopause. In addition, vaginal bleeding does not exclude pregnancy: it may be implantation bleeding, a threatened miscarriage or an ectopic pregnancy.

Pseudocyesis (false pregnancy)

In pseudocyesis the woman firmly believes that she is pregnant. She may have amenorrhoea, nausea, feel "movements" and even have an enlarged abdomen from fat, gas or lordosis. The hCG test is negative and the uterus is empty on ultrasound. The condition is often related to pressure from infertility. The diagnosis should be explained gently, the family involved, and the woman referred for counselling.

3. Pregnancy tests: hCG

All pregnancy tests detect human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG). This glycoprotein hormone is produced by the syncytiotrophoblast of the developing placenta. Its function is to keep the corpus luteum producing progesterone until the placenta takes over. The α-subunit of hCG is identical to that of LH, FSH and TSH, so pregnancy tests measure the β-subunit, which is specific to hCG.

Serum testBecomes positive 8–11 days after conception, before the period is missed. When measured as a number (quantitative test), hCG in a normal early pregnancy rises by at least 63% every 48 hours (it roughly doubles). It reaches a peak of about 100 000 IU/L at 8–10 weeks.
Urine testA bedside strip test that becomes positive at about the time of the missed period (about 4 weeks from the LMP). Early-morning urine gives the most reliable result. The test stays positive for 2–4 weeks after a miscarriage or delivery.
