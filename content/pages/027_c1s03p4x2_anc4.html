13. Birth preparedness

Birth preparedness means planning in advance for a normal birth and for a possible emergency, so that delays in reaching care are avoided.

Hospital protocol

Many women live far from a hospital and arrive only when they are in labour. By the third trimester the plan should be made with the woman and her family. It includes where she will give birth, transport and money set aside, a blood donor of her blood group, a birth companion, and when she should move closer to the hospital (for a high-risk woman, before labour starts, for example 2 weeks before the EDD). Home birth without a skilled birth attendant should be discouraged.

14. Minor complaints of pregnancy

Minor complaints are symptoms caused by the normal physiological changes of pregnancy (see 1.1). They are not dangerous, but they can be distressing. The health worker should explain the cause, give simple advice, and make sure that a serious condition is not being missed.

ComplaintCauseManagement
Nausea and vomitingHuman chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), which peaks at 8–12 weeksSmall, dry, frequent meals and ginger; an antiemetic if needed. Ketones in the urine or weight loss suggest hyperemesis gravidarum (see 2.1).
HeartburnProgesterone relaxing the oesophageal sphincter, and pressure on the stomachSmall meals, avoiding late meals, raising the head of the bed, and antacids.
Constipation and haemorrhoidsSlow bowel movement and raised venous pressurePlenty of fluids and fibre. Iron tablets may make it worse. A laxative such as lactulose may be given.
