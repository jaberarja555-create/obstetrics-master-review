19. Management: an emergency
  1. Safety first: never leave her alone with the baby · remove means of harm · assess suicide and infanticide risk (infanticide in up to 4% of untreated psychosis).
  2. Same-day psychiatry referral; admit, ideally to a mother-and-baby unit (or psychiatric ward with family support).
  3. Exclude organic causes: temperature, BP, urine protein, glucose, FBC, malaria film, electrolytes, TSH; CT if focal signs or fits.
  4. Antipsychotic: olanzapine 5–10 mg at night or haloperidol; short benzodiazepine for agitation and sleep · mood stabiliser (lithium) for mania · antidepressant if depressive.
  5. ECT: severe, life-threatening, not eating, or no response. Works fast and is safe in the puerperium.
  6. Feeding: breastfeeding is usually possible on antipsychotics; avoid it on lithium.
Exam trap

Puerperal psychosis is not "severe blues": do not send her home with reassurance. Prognosis is good (most recover fully), but recurrence after the next birth is ≈ 50%.

20. Drugs in pregnancy and breastfeeding
DrugUse and safety
SertralineSSRI of choice after birth: very little in milk · 50 mg daily, up to 200 mg
ParoxetineLow milk levels, fine in breastfeeding · avoid in early pregnancy (heart defects)
FluoxetineLong half-life, builds up in a newborn → not first choice while breastfeeding
TricyclicsImipramine, amitriptyline: low milk levels, but dangerous in overdose · avoid doxepin
AntipsychoticsOlanzapine, quetiapine, haloperidol: breastfeeding usually allowed; watch the baby for drowsiness
LithiumBest mood stabiliser for bipolar · small risk of Ebstein anomaly · check levels · high in milk → bottle-feed · toxicity risk after delivery
ValproateAvoid in women who may conceive: neural tube defects, low IQ · switch before pregnancy
BenzodiazepinesShort course only (agitation, sleep) · floppy, drowsy baby if used near birth
